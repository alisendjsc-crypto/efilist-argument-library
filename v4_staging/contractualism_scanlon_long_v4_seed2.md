Scanlonian contractualism deserves engagement in its strongest modern form, which is not the crude observation that the unborn signed nothing. Frick's ex-ante contractualism holds that principles are justified to each person by the prospects they offer, not the outcomes they deliver; Kumar's type-standpoint supplies a bearer for those prospects — "the child who will be created" as a normative position; and hypothetical consent is presented as ordinary moral currency, the same coin that licenses surgery on the unconscious, parental authority, and the risk impositions of roads and vaccines. On this form, a principle permitting procreation under good-enough conditions offers each created person a prospect no rational agent could reasonably reject, since rejection forecloses the expected benefit. Grant the objector maximin at the outset — Rawls confined it to special conditions anyway — because the rebuttal does not need it. Three failures remain, each internal to contractualism itself.

First, the standpoint has no occupant. Ex-ante justification is justification to someone: an agent who faces a prospect and can be pointed to when we ask "justifiable to whom?" Every legitimating analogy has this structure — the unconscious patient is on the table before the scalpel moves; the populations bearing traffic and vaccine risk exist before the policy runs. Creation is the one act that manufactures its own justificand. Before the decision there is no one for whom procreation is a prospect; afterward there is only someone for whom it is a fait accompli. The "ex-ante standpoint of the created" is built by projecting the outcome backward and calling the projection a party. And "rejection forecloses the expected benefit" — forecloses it from whom? Contractualism trades in complaints borne by parties; a benefit no one is deprived of grounds no complaint, on the theory's own coin.

Second, the token's veto is laundered. Scanlon's framework earned its standing by refusing aggregation: justifiability to each, not to the average. Ex-ante contractualism reintroduces aggregation inside a single life. The child who draws the catastrophic ticket raises an ex-post complaint of full force and is told her creation was justified to a standpoint that enjoyed favorable odds — a standpoint she never occupied. Reibetanz and Otsuka pressed this against ex-ante contractualism in ordinary risk cases; creation sharpens it past that literature, because there the loser at least existed among those over whom the risk was spread. Here the odds were enjoyed by nobody and the catastrophe is owned by somebody. Kumar's device, built to explain how the non-identity child can be wronged, is inverted to explain why the wretched child was not.

Third, the analogy battery disassembles. Proxy consent bridges a gap in an existing agent's agency, answerable to her when she wakes; non-treatment would harm her, while non-creation harms no one. Parental authority presupposes the ward; it cannot license the act that manufactures one. Social risk runs on reciprocity, exit, and compensation — none of which transfers, since the created child's only exit is the catastrophe the natalist books as tail risk, and no compensation answers a life that is itself the loss. The generalization: hypothetical consent legitimates where refusal also imposes on an existing subject. Creation is the unique case where refusal imposes on no one; invoking hypothetical consent there is gratuitous risk-transfer wearing justification's clothes.

What survives for the natalist: a committed Frickian may bite every bullet — types as justificands, ex-post complaints fully answered ex ante. That package is coherent, and ex-ante versus ex-post rejection is a live internal dispute. But its price should be named: justifiability-to-each becomes justifiability-to-the-average, the contract's parties become constructions no one ever is, and the one person guaranteed to exist when complaints are heard is the one whose veto has been defined away. A contractualism signed only by abstractions and enforced only on the born keeps Scanlon's name and surrenders his point.
